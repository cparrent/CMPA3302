# Use of Force Law

The use of force is one of the most important areas of law affecting police officers. Officers may encounter situations where force is necessary to make an arrest, protect another person or defend themselves. The amount of force that is legally reasonable depends on the circumstances facing the officer at the time.

## Objective Reasonableness

In *Graham v. Connor* (1989), the United States Supreme Court established that claims involving an officer's use of force should be evaluated under the Fourth Amendment's objective reasonableness standard. This means the officer's actions are considered from the perspective of a reasonable officer facing the same circumstances rather than with the benefit of hindsight.

**Source:** [Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/490/386)

Factors can include the seriousness of the suspected offense, whether the person presents an immediate threat and whether the person is actively resisting arrest or attempting to flee.

## Technology and Documentation

Technology has become increasingly important when reviewing use-of-force incidents. [[body-worn-cameras|Body-Worn Cameras and Officer Safety]] and in-car camera systems may provide video and audio recordings that help document what occurred.

However, camera footage has limitations. A camera may have a different position and field of view than the officer and may not capture everything the officer could see or hear. Video evidence should therefore be considered along with reports, physical evidence, witness statements and other available information.

## Less-Lethal Options

Modern officers may have several force options available depending on their training, agency policy and the circumstances of an encounter. [[less-lethal-technology|Less-Lethal Technology]] can provide alternatives in situations where its use is reasonable and appropriate.

Technology can assist officers and provide additional documentation, but it does not replace sound judgment, training or knowledge of the law. Officers must understand both constitutional standards and their agency's policies when making use-of-force decisions.
